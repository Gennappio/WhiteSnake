Good question! In fact, human beings do not have just one type of reasoning: the brain uses several “families” of logical processes (some slower and more reflective, others faster and more intuitive). Here is a detailed map:
 
🧠 1) Logical and deductive reasoning
•	Classical deduction:
You start from general rules → you arrive at a specific conclusion.
E.g.: “All human beings are mortal. Socrates is a man. → Socrates is mortal.”
•	Formal proofs:
They use symbols, mathematical logic, syllogisms.
•	Characteristic: If the premises are true, the conclusion is certain.
 
🧠 2) Inductive reasoning
•	You observe particular cases → you generalise to a rule.
E.g.: “I have seen many white swans → all swans are white” (which can be falsified).
•	Typical of the scientific method: accumulation of evidence, extraction of patterns.
 
🧠 3) Abductive reasoning (inference to the best explanation)
•	You start from a surprising fact → you hypothesise the most plausible explanation.
E.g.: “The lawn is wet → it probably rained.”
•	It is the heart of diagnostic thinking (medicine, investigations, etc.).
 
🧠 4) Causal reasoning
•	You analyse cause-effect relationships, often with counterfactuals:
If I remove X, does Y still happen?
•	It is used to understand causal direction (as in your example: “D→L or L→D?”).
 
🧠 5) Conditional reasoning (“if… then…”)
•	Analysis of alternative scenarios: “If I do X, does Y happen?”
•	Fundamental for planning and decision making.
•	The brain simulates scenarios with mental models (small “mental simulations”).
 
🧠 6) Analogical and metaphorical reasoning
•	You transfer knowledge from a known domain to a new one.
E.g.: “The brain works like a computer.”
•	Powerful for creativity and problem solving.
 
🧠 7) Reasoning by similarity or association
•	Faster, based on patterns:
“This situation looks like that time when… → the same thing will probably happen.”
 
🧠 8) Bayesian/probabilistic reasoning
•	They estimate probabilities instead of certainties.
•	Many cognitive processes are unconsciously Bayesian: we update beliefs with new evidence.
 
🧠 9) Heuristic reasoning
•	Mental shortcuts: useful but fallible.
Examples: availability (you judge the probability of an event by how easily it comes to mind), representativeness, etc.
 
🧠 10) Pragmatic and rhetorical reasoning
•	Based on context and on communicative effect, not only on logic.
•	This is where framing comes in: a sentence can change perception while saying the same thing.
 
🧠 11) Moral and value-based reasoning
•	They mix emotions and abstract principles.
•	They involve limbic areas + the prefrontal cortex.
•	They are not purely logical, but they guide deep choices.
 
🧠 12) Metacognitive reasoning
•	You reason about your own reasoning:
“Why do I think this way? Am I confusing cause and effect?”
•	Essential for critical thinking.

Here is an even more complete expansion:
 
🧠 1) Logical-formal reasoning (abstract level)
•	Syllogistic: use of formal syllogisms (“A implies B; B implies C; therefore A implies C”).
•	Mathematical reasoning: symbolic deductions, proofs, algebra.
•	Axiomatic reasoning: building on fixed postulates.
•	Modal reasoning: evaluates possibilities, necessities, obligations (modal logic).
 
🧠 2) Empirical and scientific reasoning
•	Statistical inference: use of numerical data to generalise.
•	Experimental reasoning: hypothesis → experiment → revision.
•	Rigorous counterfactual reasoning: imagining alternative worlds to isolate variables (“if X had not happened, would Y have happened?”).
 
🧠 3) Practical reasoning (decision making)
•	Instrumental/teleological: choices based on ends and means.
•	Economic: cost-benefit analysis, game theory.
•	Reasoning under uncertainty: assessment of risks, possible scenarios.
•	Advanced heuristics & biases: cognitive shortcuts (anchoring, framing effect, etc.).
 
🧠 4) Creative reasoning
•	Lateral (Edward de Bono): looks for non-obvious solutions, breaks logical patterns.
•	Divergent: generating many hypotheses without initial filters.
•	Synthetic: joins distant ideas into a new configuration.
•	Complex analogical: not only surface similarities, but structural ones.
 
🧠 5) Intuitive and associative reasoning
•	Pattern recognition: instant recognition of patterns (experience).
•	Semantic priming: words and concepts activate other ideas unconsciously.
•	Embodied reasoning: reasoning through bodily and sensory experiences.
 
🧠 6) Social reasoning
•	Theory of mind: inferring other people’s mental states (“they think that I think that…”).
•	Negotiation reasoning: predicting reactions, compromises.
•	Moral-social reasoning: fairness, norms, status.
 
🧠 7) Narrative reasoning
•	Story construction: we organise events into a coherent sequence.
•	Narrative coherence bias: we prefer explanations that “make a good story”, even if they are not rigorous.
 
🧠 8) Metacognitive and reflective reasoning
•	Meta-rationality: knowing when to apply rigorous logic vs intuition.
•	Epistemic: reflection on what it means to “know” and to “believe”.
•	De-biasing: strategies for recognising and correcting one’s own errors.
 
🧠 9) Ethical and philosophical reasoning
•	Deontological: right/wrong a priori (rules).
•	Consequentialist: evaluation of effects.
•	Virtue ethics: based on character and intentions.
•	Existential: questions of meaning and value.
 
🧠 10) Aesthetic and evaluative reasoning
•	Judging beauty, harmony, artistic quality: less logical, more holistic and perceptual.
 
💡 Key observation:
The brain alternates between two main systems (according to cognitive psychology):
•	System 1: fast, automatic, intuitive (pattern recognition, associations).
•	System 2: slow, deliberate, logical (deduction, critical analysis).
But in reality it is a dynamic network: logic, emotion, intuition and language work together.
 
📌 Indicative figures:
If we wanted to build a complete taxonomy, we could arrive at 30-40 subtypes of reasoning by distinguishing level (abstract, practical, creative), purpose (describing, deciding, imagining), mode (logical, intuitive, social).


🌳 Taxonomy of the types of reasoning (with simple explanations)
 
1. Logical-formal reasoning
💡 Goal: follow abstract rules and reach certain conclusions.
1.1 Deductive
•	You start from a general rule → you arrive at a specific case.
•	Example: “All cats are mammals. Luna is a cat. → Luna is a mammal.”
1.2 Syllogistic
•	Formal version of the deductive type, with sentences such as “All… some… none”.
•	Example: “All living beings breathe. Trees are living beings. → Trees breathe.”
1.3 Mathematical/Axiomatic
•	Rigorous reasoning based on symbols and postulates.
•	Example: Proving that the sum of the angles of a triangle is 180°.
1.4 Modal
•	Considers concepts such as possible, necessary, obligatory.
•	Example: “It is possible that it will rain, but it is not necessary.”
 
1.5 Inductive
•	You start from many examples → you infer a general rule (but it may be false).
•	Example: “I have only seen white swans → all swans are white.” (wrong if a black swan appears!)
 
1.6 Abduction
•	Detective reasoning: you explain a fact with the most plausible hypothesis.
•	Example: “I find the lawn wet. Probable explanation: it rained.”
 
 
2. Causal and conditional reasoning
💡 Goal: understand why something happens and what would happen if we changed something.
2.1 Direct causal
•	You find cause-effect relationships.
•	Example: “I push the glass → it falls.”
2.2 Counterfactual
•	You imagine an alternative world: “If I had not taken the train, would I have arrived late?”
•	It is used to understand true causes.
2.3 Conditional/Hypothesis (“if… then…”)
•	You simulate scenarios.
•	Example: “If I study, I pass the exam.”
2.4 Necessary vs sufficient
•	Necessary: without A there is no B.
•	Sufficient: if A is present, that is enough to have B.
•	Example: “Water is necessary to live, but not sufficient.”
2.5 Systemic
•	Reasoning about complex networks (chains of cause and effect).
•	Example: Economics: “Raising taxes reduces consumption, which reduces growth…”
 
3. Practical and decision-making reasoning
💡 Goal: choose what to do.
3.1 Teleological/Instrumental
•	You start from a goal → you look for the means.
•	Example: “I want to graduate → I have to study and pass exams.”
3.2 Economic (cost-benefit)
•	You weigh gains and losses.
•	Example: “Buying the car: it costs a lot but I save time.”
3.3 Strategic
•	You plan for the long term, as in chess.
3.4 Decision under uncertainty
•	You make decisions without having all the data.
•	Example: “I don’t know what the weather will be like; should I take the umbrella?”
3.5 Heuristic
•	Mental shortcuts: “I have always seen green lights here → I go through fast.”
•	Fast but can be wrong.
 
4. Creative and divergent reasoning
💡 Goal: find new ideas, break patterns.
4.1 Lateral
•	You change perspective to solve a problem.
•	Example: If you cannot get out of the room through the door, get out through the window.
4.2 Divergent
•	You generate lots of ideas, without judging.
•	Example: Brainstorming.
4.3 Synthetic
•	You join distant concepts into a new combination.
•	Example: Smartphone = phone + computer.
4.4 Complex analogical
•	You use deep similarities to understand new things.
•	Example: “The brain is like a network of connected cities (neurons).”
4.5 Design
•	A designer’s reasoning: creating functional and aesthetic solutions.
 
5. Intuitive and associative reasoning
💡 Goal: fast answers thanks to experience.
5.1 Pattern recognition
•	You recognise patterns: “This fever + cough = flu.”
5.2 Semantic priming
•	Words and ideas activate other ideas unconsciously.
•	Example: You read “bread” → you think “butter”.
5.3 Surface similarity
•	You rely on similar memories, even if they are not very accurate.
•	Example: “This person reminds me of a friend → I trust them.”
5.4 Embodied cognition
•	You use the body to reason.
•	Example: Counting on your fingers, using gestures to understand space.
 
6. Social reasoning
💡 Goal: understand others.
6.1 Theory of mind
•	You imagine other people’s thoughts and emotions.
•	Example: “He thinks I am angry.”
6.2 Negotiation
•	You predict other people’s moves to reach compromises.
•	Example: Negotiating a salary.
6.3 Empathic
•	Integrating other people’s emotions into choices.
•	Example: You help a sad friend.
6.4 Cultural
•	You reason on the basis of social norms.
•	Example: In Japan you bow rather than shake hands.
 
7. Narrative reasoning
💡 Goal: make sense of events as a story.
7.1 Sequential
•	You recount events in chronological order.
•	Example: “First I woke up, then I had breakfast…”
7.2 Narrative causal
•	You link events to make them coherent.
•	Example: “He lost his job because he was always late.”
7.3 Narrative bias
•	You prefer simple stories even if they are incomplete.
•	Example: Conspiracy theories: linear stories for complex events.
7.4 Identity-based
•	You use stories to define who you are.
•	Example: “I became strong because I was bullied as a child.”
 
8. Metacognitive reasoning
💡 Goal: reflect on your own thinking.
8.1 Meta-rationality
•	You choose how to reason.
•	Example: “For this decision I will use logic, not intuition.”
8.2 De-biasing
•	You recognise and correct mental errors.
•	Example: “I am judging badly because I am angry.”
8.3 Epistemic
•	You ask yourself what it means to know or to believe.
•	Example: “How do I know it is true?”
8.4 Critical-philosophical
•	You analyse deep concepts.
•	Example: “What is free will?”
 
9. Moral and ethical reasoning
💡 Goal: establish what is right/wrong.
9.1 Deontological
•	Absolute rules.
•	Example: “Stealing is always wrong.”
9.2 Consequentialist
•	You look at the consequences.
•	Example: “Lying is OK if you save a life.”
9.3 Virtue ethics
•	Character counts, not just rules.
•	Example: “An honest man would not lie.”
9.4 Contractualist
•	Shared norms for living together.
•	Example: “We obey traffic lights for safety.”
 
10. Aesthetic reasoning
💡 Goal: judge beauty and harmony.
10.1 Aesthetic perception
•	Symmetry, proportions.
•	Example: “This painting is harmonious.”
10.2 Creative-emotional
•	Based on artistic emotion.
•	Example: “This piece moves me.”
10.3 Cultural
•	Influence of tastes and fashion.
•	Example: Appreciating abstract art because it is culturally recognised.
 
11. Advanced scientific reasoning
💡 Goal: rigorous reasoning based on data.
11.1 Bayesian
•	You update beliefs with probabilities.
•	Example: “The test is positive, but the actual probability of disease is 5%.”
11.2 Statistical
•	Analysis of numerical data.
•	Example: “70% of the students passed the test.”
11.3 Experimental
•	Method: hypothesis → experiment → revision.
•	Example: Testing a new drug.
11.4 Model-based
•	You create mathematical models to predict events (climate, economy).
 
12. Specialised reasoning
💡 Goal: specific contexts.
12.1 Legal
•	You interpret laws, cases, precedents.
•	Example: Lawyers in court.
12.2 Technical/engineering
•	Analysis of complex systems.
•	Example: Designing a bridge.
12.3 Clinical
•	Medical diagnosis (abduction + experience).
•	Example: A doctor analyses symptoms.
12.4 Strategic-military
•	Planning in uncertain scenarios.
•	Example: Chess, war, geopolitics.
 
💡 In summary:
•	Logical: rigour and certainty.
•	Empirical/Scientific: observation and testing.
•	Creative: new ideas.
•	Social/Moral: relationships and ethics.
•	Metacognitive: reflection on oneself.
•	Specialised: professional applications.

You want to know whether anyone has tried to do exactly what we are doing now: a general, systematic classification of all types of human reasoning (not only logic, not only psychology, but a synthesis).
The answer is: yes, there are some philosophers and cognitive scientists who have tried to build general taxonomies.
Here are the most relevant ones:
 
📚 1. Charles Sanders Peirce (1839–1914)
•	🔑 Why he matters: He is perhaps the first to systematise three categories of reasoning:
1.	Deduction → You derive certain conclusions from premises.
2.	Induction → You generalise from cases.
3.	Abduction → The best hypotheses to explain phenomena.
•	📖 Works: “Collected Papers of Charles Sanders Peirce”.
•	🧩 Although his taxonomy is simpler than ours, it is the basis of ALL modern classifications.
 
📚 2. Stephen Toulmin (1922–2009)
•	🔑 He created the Toulmin Model of Argumentation, which analyses the structure and types of reasoning in arguments: claim, data, warrant, backing, qualifier, rebuttal.
•	Not a taxonomy of psychological “types”, but a universal framework for understanding HOW we reason and argue.
 
📚 3. Johnson-Laird and the Theory of Mental Models (1980s–today)
•	🔑 He proposes that all reasoning (deduction, counterfactual, causal, etc.) derives from a single mental mechanism: the construction of mental models in the mind.
•	They have carried out experimental studies on how we reason conditionally (“if… then…”), causally, narratively.
 
📚 4. Daniel Kahneman & Amos Tversky (1970s–2010)
•	🔑 Classification of cognitive processes into System 1 (intuitive) and System 2 (analytical).
•	They discussed dozens of cognitive biases, showing how different “types” of reasoning coexist.
•	📖 Thinking, Fast and Slow is a “practical taxonomy” of human decision-making mechanisms.
 
📚 5. Gerd Gigerenzer
•	🔑 He proposes a taxonomy of heuristics, distinguishing them from pure logical reasoning.
•	He shows that human beings use different adaptive strategies depending on the context (not a single “type of logic”).
 
📚 6. Howard Gardner – Theory of Multiple Intelligences (1983)
•	🔑 It is not a classification of “reasoning” in the strict sense, but it shows that the brain has different ways of processing: logical-mathematical, linguistic, spatial, musical, interpersonal, intrapersonal, naturalistic.
•	It implies that there are different types of reasoning tied to these intelligences.
 
📚 7. Douglas Walton (1942–2020)
•	🔑 He created a complete typology of the types of argument (causal, from analogy, from example, from authority, etc.).
•	In “Argumentation Schemes” (2008) he maps dozens of reasoning schemes, with rules and conditions.
 
📚 8. Paul Thagard – Cognitive Science of Reasoning
•	🔑 He integrated philosophy, AI, neuroscience: he proposes that human reasoning is a network of micro-strategies: analogical, causal, probabilistic, constraint-based.
•	Works: “Mind: Introduction to Cognitive Science”.
 
📚 9. Edward de Bono – Lateral Thinking
•	🔑 Not a formal taxonomy, but he clearly distinguishes vertical thinking (logical) from lateral thinking (creative), paving the way for studies of divergent reasoning.
 
📚 10. Handbooks of logic and cognitive science
•	Books such as “Cognitive Science” (Stillings et al.) and “The Cambridge Handbook of Thinking and Reasoning” (Holyoak & Morrison, 2005) offer concise maps of all types of reasoning (deduction, induction, abduction, causal, analogical, probabilistic…).
 
🔑 In summary: who has done “work like ours”
Author	What they did	Relevance to a general taxonomy
Peirce	Distinguished Deduction, Induction, Abduction	First formal classification
Walton	Argumentation schemes	Extremely rich typology
Johnson-Laird	Mental models for all reasoning	Unifying cognitive basis
Kahneman & Tversky	System 1/2, cognitive biases	Explain rational vs intuitive
Gigerenzer	Taxonomy of heuristics	Adaptive reasoning
Thagard	Unified view (logical, causal, analogical)	Modern synthesis
Toulmin	Universal schema of argumentation	Structure of all reasoning

🧩 1. Negotiation seen through game theory
Scenario:
Two people, Alice and Bob, have to split €100. Both want to maximise their gain, but if they do not reach an agreement, neither gets anything.
It is a classic “bargaining game” situation.
In game theory:
•	Players: Alice, Bob
•	Strategies: how much to ask for, how to make offers
•	Payoff: each one’s final gain
•	Equilibrium: the point at which no one can improve their position by unilaterally changing strategy (Nash equilibrium).
 
🔍 2. Reasoning primitives involved
To reason in this situation, the brain uses:
Type of reasoning (primitive)	Role in the negotiation
Logical deduction	Applying rules: “If I offer too little, he will refuse.”
Causal reasoning	“If I do X, Bob will react with Y.”
Counterfactual reasoning	“What if I asked for 60 instead of 50?”
Analogical	“This is like the negotiation to buy a house.”
Probabilistic (Bayesian)	Estimating the probabilities of reactions: “80% chance he accepts 50.”
Theory of mind (social)	Inferring intentions: “He wants to seem tough but he is in a hurry.”
Heuristic	“Let’s split it down the middle: quick solution.”
Narrative	Creating a convincing story to persuade: “If you accept now, we both win.”
So negotiation reasoning is not “new”: it is a network of combined primitives.
 
⚙️ 3. Mental algorithm of negotiation (simplified)
Let’s look at it as cognitive pseudo-code:
INPUT: Initial offer, information about the counterpart, time, goal.

1. Gather information
   - Observe signals (body language, past stories) [Analogical + Social]
   - Estimate the other party’s needs and priorities [Theory of mind]

2. Generate possible scenarios
   - List of moves (offers) [Creative/Divergent]
   - Predict the other party’s reactions [Causal + Counterfactual]

3. Evaluate probabilities and payoffs
   - Assign a probability to each reaction [Probabilistic/Bayesian]
   - Compute expected gain [Deductive/Logical]

4. Apply emotional/social constraints
   - Consider reputation, cultural norms [Cultural + Social]
   - Adjust strategy (e.g. make a concession to appear fair)

5. Choose the optimal strategy
   - Use heuristics (“let’s aim for 50/50 if time is tight”) [Heuristic]
   - Or compute the Nash equilibrium if rational [Logical-Formal]

6. Communicate and persuade
   - Present the offer with a convincing narrative [Narrative + Social]

7. Adapt dynamically
   - If refused: update the model of the counterpart [Bayesian]
   - Repeat from step 2 with new information.

OUTPUT: Optimal agreement (or failure with a lesson learned).
 
🧠 What happens in the brain
•	The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex performs logical and probabilistic calculations.
•	The amygdala and the limbic system handle emotional signals (pressure, bluffing).
•	The temporal cortex processes social signals (face, tone of voice).
•	The hippocampus recalls analogical examples (other negotiations).

Negotiation reasoning is:
A cognitive pipeline that combines deduction, causal, probabilistic and analogical reasoning, theory of mind, heuristics and narrative.
It is like a multi-module algorithm: each module is a reasoning “primitive”, and negotiation is the flow that orchestrates them to maximise payoff and cooperation.

Here is a minimal but “real” language for writing cognitive algorithms starting from the reasoning primitives. I call it RPL — Reasoning Primitives Language.
 
RPL — Reasoning Primitives Language (v0.1)
1) Data types (static, with inference)
•	Bool, Num, Str
•	Atom (symbol: Rain, Alice, Offer(60))
•	Fact = Atom | Pred(…args…) (e.g. Likes(Alice, Tea))
•	Rule = Premises => Conclusion [weight:?Num]
•	Set[T], List[T], Map[K,V]
•	Dist[T] (discrete distribution over T)
•	Model = {facts:Set[Fact], rules:Set[Rule]}
•	Agent = {beliefs:Model, priors:Map[Atom,Num]}
Useful aliases
•	Belief[T] = {value:T, conf:Num} (confidence 0..1)
•	Payoff = Num
 
2) Lexicon and identifiers
•	Names: snake_case or CamelCase
•	Variables: begin with ? (e.g. ?x, ?offer)
•	Comments: // …
•	Strings: "…". Numbers: 42, 0.75.
 
3) Basic syntax (reduced EBNF)
program      ::= { decl | stmt }
decl         ::= "let" id "=" expr
stmt         ::= expr ";"
expr         ::= literal | id | lambda | app | if | block | match
literal      ::= Num | Str | Bool | Atom | set | list | map
lambda       ::= "\" "(" params ")" "->" expr
app          ::= id "(" [args] ")"
if           ::= "if" expr "then" expr "else" expr
block        ::= "{" {decl|stmt} expr "}"
match        ::= "match" expr "{" { case } "}"
case         ::= "case" pattern "->" expr
pattern      ::= literal | id | "_" | Pred(pattern, …)
args         ::= expr { "," expr }
params       ::= id ":" type { "," id ":" type }
type         ::= basic | generic
 
4) Reasoning primitives (signature + intuition)
Logical-epistemic
•	DED(rule:Rule, facts:Set[Fact]) : Fact|Set[Fact]
Applies rules to facts.
•	IND(cases:Set[Fact]) : Rule
Induces a rule from the cases (greedy, weighted).
•	ABD(obs:Set[Fact], model:Model) : Set[Hypothesis]
Hypotheses that explain the observations.
Causal/conditional
•	CAU(x:Atom, y:Atom, data:Set[Fact]) : Belief[Link]
Estimates the causal link (confidence).
•	COND(if:Fact, then:Fact) : Rule
•	CF(world:Model, change:Set[Fact], query:Fact) : Belief[Fact]
Counterfactual: evaluates query in the modified world.
Probabilistic
•	PROB(event:Atom, prior:Num, likelihoods:Map[Atom,Num]) : Num
Simple Bayes (returns the posterior).
•	UPDATE(dist:Dist[Atom], evidence:Atom) : Dist[Atom]
Analogical / structural
•	ANA(src:Model, tgt:Model, map_hint:Map[Atom,Atom]) : Map[Atom,Atom]
Analogical mapping (returns correspondences).
Social / narrative / meta
•	SOC(agent:Agent) : Belief[Map[Atom,Num]]
What the other party believes/values.
•	NAR(events:List[Fact], goal:Atom) : Str
Minimal persuasive text.
•	META(proof:any) : Str
Explains why a conclusion was chosen.
Utility
•	ARGMAX(xs:List[T], f:\(T)->Num) : T
•	EXPECT(p:Num, win:Num, lose:Num) : Num (expected value p*win+(1-p)*lose)
 
5) Semantics (intuitive, deterministic where possible)
•	DED: applies rules by forward chaining until there are no new facts (closure limited to the scope of the block).
•	IND: generates a Rule with confidence = coverage of correct cases / total.
•	ABD: proposes minimal hypotheses whose DED explains obs; ranks by parsimony (Occam) and conf.
•	CF: creates a copy of the world with change, then runs DED/PROB, returns Belief.
•	PROB/UPDATE: Bayesian normalisation over finite sets.
•	ANA: looks for rough graph isomorphisms between predicates and relations (heuristic).
 
6) Mini–standard library (useful rules)
let payoff_of(offer:Num, accept_prob:Num) =
  offer * accept_prob;

let fairness_baseline = 50;

let accept_rule =
  COND( Offer(?x) & ?x >= 50 , Accept ); // toy example

let bargain_model : Model = {
  facts = {},
  rules = { accept_rule }
};
 
7) Complete example: negotiation (bargaining over €100)
Goal
Alice has to choose an offer to make to Bob. If Bob accepts, Alice’s payoff = offer; if he refuses, 0.
// ── Initial data
let offers = [40, 50, 60, 70];          // action space
let prior_accept = Map{ 40:0.3, 50:0.7, 60:0.5, 70:0.2 };

let bob : Agent = {
  beliefs = bargain_model,
  priors  = Map{ Accept:0.5 }
};

// ── Probabilistic estimate of acceptance with a “very crude” Bayes
let estimate_accept = \(o:Num) -> {
  // Simplified likelihood via deduction from a fairness rule
  let fair = (o >= fairness_baseline);
  let like = if fair then 0.8 else 0.3;
  let prior = prior_accept[o];
  PROB(Accept, prior, Map{ Evidence: like })
};

// ── Expected value for each offer
let expected_payoff = \(o:Num) -> {
  let p = estimate_accept(o);
  payoff_of(o, p)
};

// ── Optimal choice (greedy on expected value)
let best_offer = ARGMAX(offers, expected_payoff);

// ── Persuasive narrative (fairness + urgency framing)
let pitch = NAR(
  [ Fact("MutualBenefit"), Fact("TimeCost") ],
  Goal("AgreeFast")
);

// ── Final output
{ best_offer, expected_payoff(best_offer), pitch }
What the program does
1.	It uses a fairness heuristic for the likelihood (o>=50 ⇒ acceptance more likely).
2.	Bayes updates the probability of acceptance for each offer.
3.	It computes the expected value and chooses the ARGMAX.
4.	It generates a narrative to present the offer.
 
8) Example: medical diagnosis (ABD + PROB + DED)
let symptoms = { Fever, Cough };
let med_model : Model = {
  facts = {},
  rules = {
    COND( Flu , Fever & Cough ),
    COND( Covid, Fever & Cough & LossSmell )
  }
};

// Hypotheses that explain the observed symptoms
let hyps = ABD(symptoms, med_model); // → { Flu, Covid }

// Update probabilities with a negative rapid Covid test
let post_flu   = PROB(Flu,   0.10, Map{ TestNeg: 0.9 });
let post_covid = PROB(Covid, 0.08, Map{ TestNeg: 0.95 });

// Choose a guideline-driven treatment
let treat = if post_flu >= post_covid
            then DED(Guideline(Flu), symptoms)
            else DED(Guideline(Covid), symptoms);
 
9) Example: legal counterfactual check (CF)
let case_world : Model = {
  facts = { Speeding, Rain, Crash },
  rules = { COND(Speeding & Rain, Crash) }
};

// Would the Crash have happened without Speeding?
let alt = CF(case_world, { not Speeding }, Crash);
// alt : Belief[Fact] with confidence ~ low ⇒ Speeding is a necessary cause
 
10) Traceability (explanations)
Any primitive can return a proof object. With META you get an explanation:
let concl = DED(accept_rule, { Offer(60) });
let why   = META(concl);   // "Applied rule accept_rule with premise Offer(60)…"
 
11) Errors and types (minimal checker)
•	DED requires Rules compatible with the input Facts.
•	PROB returns Num ∈ [0,1]; UPDATE always normalises.
•	CF works on copies of Model (immutability by default).
•	ARGMAX requires a non-empty list (otherwise Option[T]).
 
12) Extensibility (macros & modules)
•	Macros: define Walton-style argumentation schemes as functions:
let from_authority = \(claim:Fact, expert:Atom, domain:Atom) ->
  COND( Expert(expert, domain) & Says(expert, claim), claim );
•	Modules: import prob, import causal, import walton_schemes.

1️⃣ Complete Grammar (BNF)
<program>       ::= { <decl> | <stmt> }

<decl>          ::= "let" <id> "=" <expr>
<stmt>          ::= <expr> ";"

<expr>          ::= <literal> 
                  | <id> 
                  | <lambda>
                  | <app>
                  | <if>
                  | <block>
                  | <match>

<lambda>        ::= "\" "(" <params> ")" "->" <expr>
<params>        ::= <id> ":" <type> { "," <id> ":" <type> }

<app>           ::= <id> "(" [<args>] ")"
<args>          ::= <expr> { "," <expr> }

<if>            ::= "if" <expr> "then" <expr> "else" <expr>

<block>         ::= "{" {<decl> | <stmt>} <expr> "}"

<match>         ::= "match" <expr> "{" { <case> } "}"
<case>          ::= "case" <pattern> "->" <expr>
<pattern>       ::= <literal> | <id> | "_" | <pred>

<literal>       ::= <num> | <str> | <bool> | <atom> 
                  | <set> | <list> | <map>

<set>           ::= "{" [<expr> {"," <expr>}] "}"
<list>          ::= "[" [<expr> {"," <expr>}] "]"
<map>           ::= "Map" "{" [<map_entry> {"," <map_entry>}] "}"
<map_entry>     ::= <expr> ":" <expr>

<atom>          ::= <upper_id> [ "(" [<args>] ")" ]
<pred>          ::= <atom>

<type>          ::= "Num" | "Bool" | "Str" | "Atom" 
                  | "Fact" | "Rule" | "Model" | "Agent"
                  | "Dist" "<" <type> ">"
                  | "List" "<" <type> ">"
                  | "Set" "<" <type> ">"
                  | "Map" "<" <type> "," <type> ">"
                  | <id>

<id>            ::= <lower_letter> {<letter> | <digit> | "_"}
<upper_id>      ::= <upper_letter> {<letter> | <digit> | "_"}
<num>           ::= <digit>+ ["." <digit>+]
<str>           ::= '"' {<char>} '"'
<bool>          ::= "true" | "false"

<letter>        ::= "a" | ... | "z" | "A" | ... | "Z"
<digit>         ::= "0" | ... | "9"
<char>          ::= any printable character
🔑 Note: this grammar is minimalist but Turing-complete: it has functions, applications, data structures.
 
2️⃣ Mini-interpreter (Operational Specifications)
Let us define how the language executes code.
Think of a functional interpreter, immutable by default.
 
Execution environment
•	Env: map from identifiers → values.
•	Value: can be Num, Str, Bool, Atom, List, Set, Map, Lambda, Fact, Rule, Model, etc.
 
Expression evaluation (small-step semantics)
⟦x⟧env           = lookup(env, x)
⟦n⟧env           = n
⟦"text"⟧env      = "text"

⟦let x = e1; e2⟧env = ⟦e2⟧(env[x ↦ ⟦e1⟧env])

⟦λ(p).e⟧env       = closure(p,e,env)
⟦f(arg)⟧env      = apply(⟦f⟧env, ⟦arg⟧env)

⟦if e1 then e2 else e3⟧env = if ⟦e1⟧env then ⟦e2⟧env else ⟦e3⟧env

⟦{a,b,c}⟧env     = Set{⟦a⟧,⟦b⟧,⟦c⟧}
⟦[a,b,c]⟧env     = List[⟦a⟧,⟦b⟧,⟦c⟧]
⟦Map{a:b}⟧env    = Map{⟦a⟧:⟦b⟧}
 
Primitives (intuitive semantics)
Primitive	Semantics
DED(rules,facts)	Forward-chaining algorithm: applies rules until no new facts emerge.
IND(cases)	Frequency analysis: generates a Rule with confidence = countTrue / total.
ABD(obs,model)	Abductive search: generates a minimal set of hypotheses that explain obs under model.
CAU(x,y,data)	Causality: evaluates dependence `P(y
CF(world,change,query)	Copies world, applies change, runs DED, computes the probability of query.
PROB(event,prior,likelihoods)	Bayes: posterior = (likelihood*prior)/Z.
ANA(src,tgt)	Graph matching: looks for isomorphisms between src and tgt.
SOC(agent)	Estimates the agent’s beliefs (using priors and models).
NAR(events,goal)	Generates a simple narrative sentence: "Because X,Y -> Goal".
META(obj)	Generates an explanation of the derivation (proof tree).
 
Execution steps
1.	Parsing → AST according to the BNF.
2.	Type-checking: checks function signatures, data types (Num, Rule, etc.).
3.	Evaluation: immutable functional model, binding environment, closure application.
4.	Primitive reasoning engine:
o	DED/IND/ABD use propositional logic algorithms.
o	CF creates copies of models (world) with modifications.
o	PROB implements Bayes.
5.	Output: value or Belief[value,confidence].
 
3️⃣ RPL Manual (2 pages)
 
🌐 What RPL is
Reasoning Primitives Language is a minimal language for modelling human reasoning as compositions of cognitive primitives: deduction, induction, abduction, causal, probabilistic, analogical, narrative, social, meta.
Goal: describe complex cognitive algorithms (negotiations, diagnoses, legal decisions) with basic building blocks.
 
📦 Main types
Type	Meaning
Fact	Atomic facts: Rain, Offer(60)
Rule	Logical rule: COND(Premises, Conclusion)
Model	Set of facts and rules
Agent	Beliefs and priors of an actor
Dist[T]	Probability distribution over T
Belief[T]	Value + confidence (0..1)
 
🛠️ Cognitive primitives (API)
Name	Signature	Description
DED	(rules:Set[Rule], facts:Set[Fact]) → Set[Fact]	Forward chaining.
IND	(cases:Set[Fact]) → Rule	Induction.
ABD	(obs:Set[Fact], model:Model) → Set[Hypothesis]	Diagnosis of plausible hypotheses.
CAU	(x:Atom, y:Atom, data:Set[Fact]) → Belief[Link]	Causality.
CF	(world:Model, change:Set[Fact], query:Fact) → Belief[Fact]	Counterfactuals.
PROB	(event:Atom, prior:Num, likelihoods:Map[Atom,Num]) → Num	Bayes.
ANA	(src:Model, tgt:Model, hint:Map[Atom,Atom]) → Map[Atom,Atom]	Structural analogy.
SOC	(agent:Agent) → Belief[Map[Atom,Num]]	Theory of mind.
NAR	(events:List[Fact], goal:Atom) → Str	Persuasive narrative.
META	(obj:any) → Str	Proof explanation.

🧩 Architecture
•	Functional, immutable interpreter.
•	Logic engine: forward/backward chaining.
•	Probability: discrete Bayes.
•	Counterfactuals: copy and mutate Model.
•	Analogical: graph matching.
•	Meta: proof tree.
 
🎯 Philosophy
RPL is cognitive assembly:
•	A few universal primitives (logical, probabilistic, analogical, narrative).
•	Complex algorithms = combinations (negotiation, diagnosis, legal).
•	Every expression can be explained with META.
